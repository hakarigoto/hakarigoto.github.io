Why low mileage is expensive

The costs that dominate car ownership do not care how much you drive. Insurance renews, registration comes due, the vehicle depreciates sitting in a driveway, and a parking space costs the same in a month you barely leave the house. Fuel — the cost most people think of first — is often the smallest line.

That is why the per-mile figure above matters more than the total. Someone paying $6,000 a year in fixed costs and driving 3,000 miles is spending about $2 a mile before they buy a drop of gas. At 15,000 miles a year, the same car costs about 40 cents a mile. Same vehicle, same bills, completely different value.

Almost nobody replaces a car with one thing. The realistic pattern is transit for the commute, rideshare for the gaps, and a rental for the trips that need a trunk. The calculator models that mix in the third row.

One honest caveat: that mix only exists where transit exists. Outside a handful of metros — New York, Chicago, Boston, DC, San Francisco and a few others — bus and rail coverage in the US ranges from thin to nonexistent, especially in suburbs and smaller cities. If that is your situation, answer “No” to the transit question above and the calculator will compare only the options actually available to you.

What the dollars don't tell you

  • Coverage. Rideshare availability and wait times drop off fast outside dense areas. Check the app at the hours you actually travel, not at noon on a weekday.
  • Surge pricing. The per-mile rate you enter above is an average. Storms, rush hour and event nights are when you most need a ride and when it costs the most.
  • Trips you can't shift. A large grocery run, a child's car seat, medical appointments, a shift that ends after transit stops — these decide the question more often than the arithmetic does.
  • Selling the car. If you owe more than the vehicle is worth, selling does not free you from the loan. Check your payoff amount first.

Common questions

At how many miles per year does giving up a car stop making sense?

With roughly $6,000 in annual fixed costs and rideshare around $2 per mile, the break-even lands near 3,000 miles a year. Your parking and insurance can move that number a long way in either direction, so use your own figures above.

What counts as a fixed cost?

Anything you pay whether or not you drive: insurance, registration, inspection, routine maintenance, tires, depreciation, and loan or lease payments if you still have them.

Does this include car payments?

Only if you enter them in the fixed cost field. This tool compares running costs; it does not model loan payoff or resale value.

Is going car-free realistic in a suburb?

Cost is only half the question. Check rideshare wait times where you live at the hours you travel, and be honest about the trips that would be hard to replace.

What if there's no public transit where I live?

Then the realistic comparison is owning versus rideshare only — choose “No” on the transit question and the tool drops the transit row. In transit-poor areas, also look at carpooling or employer vanpools, which this calculator does not model.

What if I don't have a driver's license?

Then compare the alternatives against each other rather than against ownership. Set the ownership row aside and look at which mix of transit, rideshare and rentals costs least.

How the calculation works
  • Ownership: annual fixed costs + (monthly parking × 12) + (miles ÷ MPG × gas price)
  • Rideshare only: miles × rideshare rate per mile
  • Transit + rideshare: (transit pass × 12) + (miles not covered by transit × rideshare rate). Shown only if you say usable transit exists where you live.
  • Break-even mileage: the annual mileage at which ownership and rideshare-only cost the same, solved as fixed costs ÷ (rideshare rate − fuel cost per mile)
  • Not included: vehicle resale value, loan payoff, surge pricing, car rentals, tolls, tickets, and the value of your time
  • Default values are editable starting points, not recommendations. Replace them with your own figures.
